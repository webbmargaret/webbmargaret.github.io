A DATA REPORT FOR THE DISCIPLINE-BASED EDUCATION RESEARCH (DBER) COMMUNITY

Tracking the Postdoc Pipeline

Fifteen-plus years of job postings from five community-run boards — PER Jobs (physics), Engineering Education, AMTE (math ed), SABER (biology ed), and a chemistry faculty jobs list — scraped, classified, and read together.

By Dr. Margaret Webb (LinkedIn), Dr. Alexandra Coso Strong, and Alexandra Werth — Cornell University

Data through — · — listings classified · Updated

A note on why this exists

From two boards to five — still mostly alone

This started as a two-board comparison: PER Jobs and the EER wiki, the closest things physics and engineering education had to a shared job board. Since then, three more have joined — AMTE's math-ed listings, SABER's biology-ed listings, and a hand-curated chemistry faculty jobs list. That said, most DBER postings still circulate on listservs, X/Twitter threads, and word of mouth, which means people miss openings simply for being in the wrong subfield's social circle.

  • Know a job board for another Discipline-Based Education Research (DBER) subfield? Tell us — we'll add it to the pipeline and start tracking it here.
  • Don't have one for your field? The scraper and classifier are built to be forked — point them at a new URL and a new board is a few hours of work, not a few months. Three of the five boards here (AMTE, SABER, Chemistry) were added exactly that way.
  • Want to go further? The shared position-type taxonomy across fields — not a merged board, just a common vocabulary — is now live across all five sources, so cross-field comparisons in §5–§8 are apples-to-apples where the underlying job markets are actually comparable (see the caveats in §12 for where they aren't).

§1 · Overview

Five boards, one shared question

Since the mid-2000s, two volunteer-run pages have quietly served as the closest thing discipline-based education research (DBER) and engineering education research (EER) have to a shared job board: the PER Jobs blog, covering physics and science education research broadly, and the Engineering Education Community Resource wiki, focused on engineering education. Neither site was built for data analysis; they're primarily lists, updated by hand, aimed at people looking for their next position. This report started with those two.

It now also tracks three more, each recovered a different way. AMTE's math-education job listings and SABER's biology-education job listings don't keep their own historical archives — old postings just disappear — so both had to be reconstructed from the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine, walking every saved snapshot of each page and stitching the fragments back into a timeline. The chemistry faculty jobs list is different again: a hand-curated, crowdsourced spreadsheet tracking chemistry faculty searches specifically, not scraped from a live site at all, and normalized here from a headerless CSV whose column layout drifted across roughly three eras of the underlying sheet.

This report scrapes/loads all five, classifies every listing into a shared position type, and reads the datasets side by side: what's being posted, how much of it is postdoctoral, how that's shifted over time, and how much of that shift survives once you account for how much of each dataset is genuinely ambiguous, how thin some year-slices are, or — for Wayback-reconstructed sources — how much history simply wasn't archived in the first place.

Every chart below is interactive — hover for exact values, and where a number is an estimate rather than a count, there's a band or whisker showing how much to trust it.

This report grew out of a companion critical literature review, Building Capacity for Instructional Change: A Critical Review and Conceptual Framework for Discipline-Based Education Research (DBER) Postdoctoral Professional Development (Webb, Coso Strong, & Werth, presented at the 2026 ASEE Annual Conference & Exposition, Charlotte, NC, Paper #50607). That paper cites the original PER + EER job-board data to motivate its central argument: growth in DBER postdoctoral positions doesn't by itself guarantee more capacity for instructional change in STEM classrooms — that capacity has to be built relationally, through the tensions and support structures postdocs navigate with faculty collaborators. The paper proposes a conceptual framework for how that happens; the PER + EER combined trend in §6 is the specific data behind its motivating claims, kept current here. The three additional boards were added afterward, extending the same infrastructure to more of the DBER community rather than revising the paper's own claims. (Paper available on request until it's publicly indexed.)

§10 · How to Read the Uncertainty

Three different bands, three different meanings

This report uses uncertainty visualization in three different ways, deliberately not conflated into one generic "error bar," because they answer different questions:

WhereWhat it showsWhat it means
Fig. 2 hatched segmentsShare of listings classified Unknown / OtherClassifier coverage — how much of that bar's total is not confidently placed into any category
Fig. 4 shaded bandRolling standard deviation around the 5-yr meanVolatility — how much raw year-to-year counts vary within the smoothing window
Figs. 5 & 6 whiskers95% confidence interval for a proportionSampling uncertainty — how precisely a percentage is known given that year's total number of listings

None of these are error bars in the classical repeated-measurement sense — there was one scrape (or, for AMTE/SABER, one Wayback reconstruction; for Chemistry, one load of the source spreadsheet), not repeated trials. They're the closest statistically honest analogue available for observational count data like this, and apply the same way across all five sources.

§11 · Methodology

How this was built

1. Scraping — PER & EER

PER Jobs is a Blogspot blog, so the scraper walks its date-grouped post structure page by page, following the "Older Posts" link until it runs out, recording each post's title and the date header above it. The EER wiki is a PBworks page, edited in place rather than posted chronologically — so instead of one scrape, the EER scraper visits every saved revision of the page (over 500 of them) and extracts the job table from each, treating a job's first appearance across revisions as its posting date. This is slower and messier than a blog scrape, but it's the only way to recover history from a wiki that doesn't keep dated posts.

2. Reconstructing AMTE & SABER from the Wayback Machine

Neither AMTE's math-ed board nor SABER's biology-ed board keeps its own history — a listing simply disappears once it expires. Both were rebuilt by querying the Internet Archive's CDX API for every saved snapshot of the relevant URL, fetching each one, and parsing whatever page shape that snapshot happened to be. That "whatever page shape" turned out to matter a lot:

AMTE uses three distinct layouts across its ~175 usable snapshots (2010–2026). The oldest (2009–2013) is a plain Title / College: X / Review Date: X block with no separate poster contact info. A middle era serves its content as a JavaScript-escaped string rather than real HTML — the archived response literally contains text like \x3cp class=\"job-meta\"\x3e in place of an actual <p class="job-meta"> tag, apparently emitted via a client-side document.write() of a JS string literal — so parsing it first requires decoding that hex-escaped text back into real markup before a normal HTML parser can do anything with it. The modern layout (2024+) uses a conventional Posted by NAME (email) on DATE. Review of applicants begins DATE. sentence, which itself turned out to be split across multiple sibling DOM elements rather than sitting in one text node, requiring the parser to stitch fragments back together rather than assume one clean line per field. Where a listings page paginates beyond the snapshot's first page, the scraper also attempts to follow "next page" links, re-querying the CDX API for each subsequent page as its own archived URL — recovering additional history where Wayback happened to archive it, though this is necessarily partial (see §12).

SABER spans a mid-history platform migration. The older Wild Apricot era renders its board as a discussion forum (Topic / Last message / Replies), not a job-card layout — each row has two separate links (the topic title, and a separate "jump to last message" permalink whose visible text is just a timestamp), which an early version of this parser confused, until fixed by explicitly rejecting date-only anchor text and climbing further up the DOM tree to find the row containing both. The newer mcjobboard.net era is an Angular application that embeds its listings as literal JSON inside a <script id="serverApp-state"> tag — the same API response used to render the page, decoded with a custom short-entity scheme this site uses in place of standard HTML entities (&q; for a quote, &a; for an ampersand, etc.) — which, once decoded, is far more reliable than parsing rendered card markup.

3. Normalizing the Chemistry faculty jobs list

Unlike the other four sources, Chemistry was not scraped by this pipeline at all — it's a pre-existing, hand-curated/crowdsourced spreadsheet tracking chemistry faculty searches, loaded here from its raw CSV export. That export has no header row, and its column layout drifts across roughly three eras of the underlying sheet (an added "PhD-granting type" column partway through its history, then a further-added leading "date" column in the most recent era). Rather than hardcode three separate column-offset schemes, the normalizer locates a small controlled category token (PhD / PUI / MS / TEACH) wherever it actually falls in a given row and reads institution/department/field/rank/city/state relative to that position — one rule that handles all observed eras, recovering a usable institution field for over 99.8% of rows (4765 of 4770).

4. Date handling

PER Jobs dates come cleanly off the blog's own date headers. EER listings carry two dates: a free-text posting_date field typed by whoever edited the wiki (inconsistent formats, sometimes partial like "2018-11" or missing entirely — about 8% of rows) and a revision_date, the ISO date PBworks recorded when that row first appeared in a saved revision. This report uses revision_date throughout for EER, since it's complete and consistently formatted. AMTE/SABER use each listing's own posted date where the archived page provided one, falling back to that snapshot's crawl date otherwise. Chemistry rows carry either an explicit posted date (recent era) or an academic hiring-cycle string like "16-17" (older era, read as calendar year 2016) — never both on the same row, so there's no ambiguity about which applies to a given listing, just two different granularities across eras.

5. Classification

All five sources are classified with the same core approach: an ordered list of regular-expression rules, checked top to bottom, first match wins. Postdoc and graduate-student patterns are checked before tenure-track/professor patterns specifically because titles like "Postdoc to TT Position" or mixed pipeline listings need the postdoc signal to win. PER's classifier carries extra patterns tuned to that board's heavy use of abbreviations (TT, NTT, VAP). AMTE and SABER titles are classified the same way as PER/EER, against a shared free-text taxonomy. Chemistry has no free-text title field at all — only an abbreviated rank code (asst, assoc/full, chair, open, …) — so it uses a dedicated lookup-plus-substring classifier tuned to that specific vocabulary rather than the sentence-level rules built for the other four. Anything matching none of the rules falls into Unknown / Other — — of PER listings, — of EER listings, — of AMTE, — of SABER, — of Chemistry.

6. "Temporary / contingent" definition

For Fig. 6 and the summary in §12, a listing counts as temporary/contingent if its position type is one of:

Everything else — tenure-track, full/senior professor, department chair/program director, research scientist, staff and administrative roles, journal editors, program officers, and (for AMTE/SABER) K-12 teaching positions — is treated as non-contingent. K-12 teaching jobs sit outside this definition deliberately: it's a different labor market with its own employment norms, not comparable to higher-ed contingency in the way the other categories are. This is one reasonable line to draw, not the only one; professor-of-practice and similarly-titled non-tenure roles that are often functionally permanent are not included here, so this measure likely understates total non-tenure-track employment while giving a clean read on explicitly time-limited positions.

§12 · Limitations

What this report doesn't do

  • No deduplication between boards. All five sources were scraped/loaded and classified independently; a job cross-posted to more than one would count once per board in any cross-board figure. Based on manual review at the time this project began, overlap between PER and EER appeared to be small (a large majority of listings on each board don't appear on the other), but that estimate wasn't recomputed for the current data or extended to the three newer sources.
  • 2026 is incomplete for all sources. PER/EE were last scraped in early April 2026; AMTE/SABER/Chemistry data collection wrapped up around the same period. Anything involving 2026 — bars, points, or its contribution to rolling windows — will fill in as the year continues; treat it as a floor, not a final count.
  • AMTE and SABER are Wayback Machine reconstructions, not live scrapes — treat both as a floor on true historical volume, not a ceiling. Neither board keeps its own archive, so this data exists only because the Internet Archive happened to crawl each page at some set of moments in time. A listing posted and removed entirely between two snapshots is invisible to this pipeline and always will be. SABER's coverage is particularly sparse and short (2019–2025, and unevenly across even those years); AMTE's is longer (2010–2026) but still snapshot-dependent, and its pagination-recovery (§11) only reaches pages Wayback happened to archive separately from page one. Gaps in Figs. 1 and 3 for these two sources should be read as "the archive doesn't have this," not "nothing was posted."
  • Chemistry has a different, unverified provenance. Unlike the other four boards, this pipeline didn't scrape Chemistry — it loaded a pre-existing, hand-curated/crowdsourced spreadsheet whose original collection methodology, completeness, and update cadence aren't documented by this project. It's treated here as a good-faith community resource, not a source this pipeline can independently vouch for the completeness of the way it can for a scrape it ran itself.
  • Chemistry's near-zero postdoc/contingent share is a scope artifact, not a market signal. The list tracks chemistry faculty searches specifically. Its ~0% postdoc and contingent-faculty shares in Figs. 2, 4, 5, and 6 say nothing about whether chemistry departments actually hire postdocs or adjuncts elsewhere (they clearly do) — only that this particular list doesn't track those position types. Comparing Chemistry's tenure-track share directly against PER/EE/AMTE/SABER's is comparing a faculty-only list to boards that list everything.
  • The classifier is rule-based, not learned. Regex keyword matching is transparent and auditable (you can read every rule in the source), but it will misfire on unusual phrasing, and "first match wins" ordering means edge cases sometimes land in a defensible-but-debatable category. The Unknown / Other share (§11) is the honest measure of how often it simply gives up. Chemistry's classifier works off an abbreviated rank code rather than a full sentence, which is more constrained but also has less context to work with when a row's fields are misaligned (a small, known issue affecting well under 1% of Chemistry rows — see §11).
  • Institution-level analysis excludes PER only. PER Jobs titles are free text without a structured institution field, so Fig. 7 has no PER equivalent. The other four sources (EER, AMTE, SABER, Chemistry) all have a usable institution field and are ranked in §9.
  • All five boards are volunteer- or community-maintained and self-selecting. None is a comprehensive registry of every opening in its field — each reflects what people chose to post, and posting norms (what gets listed, how promptly) may themselves have shifted over each board's history in ways this data can't separate from real changes in the underlying job market.

§13 · Code & Reproducibility

Scraper & classifier source

Full scripts are linked below and reproduced in part here. Everything here — scraping, classification, statistics, and this page — was built to be re-run: change the classification rules, re-scrape for more recent postings, or point the same pipeline at another job board.

perjobs_scraper_alljobs.py Blogspot pagination scraper — PER Jobs 
"""
PER Jobs Blog Scraper
=====================
Scrapes all job postings from the PER Jobs blog (perjobs.blogspot.com).
Extracts job titles and posting dates, saves to CSV, and prints summary statistics.

To scrape only a specific label (e.g. postdoc), set LABEL below.
Leave LABEL as None (or "") to scrape the full blog (all jobs).

Author(s): Dr. Margaret Webb authorizing and reviewing Claude Code
Date: April 2026
"""

import requests
from bs4 import BeautifulSoup
import pandas as pd
from datetime import datetime
import time
import re

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Configuration
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Set to a label string (e.g. "postdoc") to filter, or None for ALL jobs.
LABEL = None

OUTPUT_FILENAME = "perjobs_listings.csv"

DELAY_SECONDS = 0.5


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Helpers
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

def parse_date(date_string):
    """
    Convert "Tuesday, December 2, 2025" → "2025-12-02".
    Returns None if parsing fails.
    """
    try:
        date_without_day = re.sub(r'^\w+,\s*', '', date_string.strip())
        parsed_date = datetime.strptime(date_without_day, "%B %d, %Y")
        return parsed_date.strftime("%Y-%m-%d")
    except ValueError as e:
        print(f"Warning: Could not parse date '{date_string}': {e}")
        return None


def scrape_page(url, session):
    """
    Scrape a single blog page and return (jobs_list, next_page_url_or_None).
    Each job dict has keys: title, date.
    """
    jobs = []

    response = session.get(url)
    response.raise_for_status()

    soup = BeautifulSoup(response.text, 'html.parser')

    current_date = None

    main_content = soup.find('div', class_='blog-posts')
    if main_content:
        date_sections = main_content.find_all('div', class_='date-outer')

        for section in date_sections:
            date_header = section.find('h2', class_='date-header')
            if date_header:
                date_span = date_header.find('span')
                if date_span:
                    current_date = parse_date(date_span.get_text())

            post_titles = section.find_all('h3', class_='post-title')

            for title_element in post_titles:
                link = title_element.find('a')
                title = link.get_text(strip=True) if link else title_element.get_text(strip=True)

                if title and current_date:
                    jobs.append({'title': title, 'date': current_date})

    # Pagination: find the "Older Posts" link
    next_page_url = None
    older_link = soup.find('a', class_='blog-pager-older-link')
    if older_link and older_link.get('href'):
        next_page_url = older_link.get('href')

    return jobs, next_page_url

# Main scrape loop
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

def scrape_all_jobs(label=LABEL, delay_seconds=DELAY_SECONDS):
    """
    Scrape all job postings from the PER Jobs blog.

    Args:
        label: If provided, scrape only that label (e.g. "postdoc").
               Pass None or "" to scrape the entire blog.
        delay_seconds: Polite wait between page requests.

    Returns:
        Pandas DataFrame with columns: title, date
    """
    if label:
        base_url = f"https://perjobs.blogspot.com/search/label/{label}"
        print(f"Scraping PER Jobs blog — label: '{label}'")
    else:
        base_url = "https://perjobs.blogspot.com/"
        print("Scraping PER Jobs blog — ALL jobs (no label filter)")

    session = requests.Session()
    session.headers.update({
        'User-Agent': 'PERJobsScraper/1.0 (Educational Research Project)'
    })

    all_jobs = []
    current_url = base_url
    page_count = 0

    print(f"Starting URL: {base_url}")
    print(f"Delay between requests: {delay_seconds}s")
    print("-" * 50)

    while current_url:
        page_count += 1
        print(f"Scraping page {page_count}: {current_url[:80]}...")

        try:
            jobs, next_url = scrape_page(current_url, session)
            all_jobs.extend(jobs)
            print(f"  Found {len(jobs)} jobs on this page (Total: {len(all_jobs)})")
            current_url = next_url
            if current_url:
                time.sleep(delay_seconds)

        except requests.RequestException as e:
            print(f"Error scraping page: {e}")
            break

    print("-" * 50)
    print(f"Scraping complete! Found {len(all_jobs)} total jobs.")

    df = pd.DataFrame(all_jobs)
    return df

amte_wayback_scraper.py The JS-hex-escape decoder — a real discovery, not a design choice (see §11) 
def dejs_escape(text):
    """
    Some legacy (2015-era) AMTE snapshots serve their entire job-listings
    section as a JAVASCRIPT-ESCAPED STRING rather than real HTML tags --
    confirmed via a real debug run: the raw response text contains
    literal sequences like \x3cp class=\"job-meta\"\x3ePosted by...
    (hex escapes for '<'/'>' , backslash-escaped quotes, literal \n for
    newlines) instead of an actual <p class="job-meta"> tag. This is
    almost certainly emitted via a client-side document.write() of a JS
    string literal. BeautifulSoup sees \x3cp as plain text, not a tag --
    so there's no real DOM to walk here until this is decoded back into
    genuine HTML first. Applied globally and unconditionally: safe for
    normal (2024+) pages too, since real HTML essentially never contains
    literal backslash-x-hex sequences outside this exact context, so this
    is a no-op on pages that don't need it.
    """
    text = re.sub(r"\\x([0-9a-fA-F]{2})", lambda m: chr(int(m.group(1), 16)), text)
    text = text.replace('\\"', '"')
    text = text.replace("\\/", "/")
    text = text.replace("\\n", "\n")
    text = text.replace("\\t", "\t")
    return text
saber_wayback_scraper.py mcjobboard.net's custom short-entity decoder (see §11) — confirmed against real archived output, not standard HTML escaping 
def decode_mcjobboard_short_entities(text):
    """
    mcjobboard's Angular Universal state is NOT standard-HTML-escaped.
    Confirmed via a debug run against real archived output: it uses a
    compact, non-standard scheme that keeps only the first letter of
    each entity name -- &quot; -> &q;, &lt; -> &l;, &gt; -> &g;,
    &amp; -> &a; -- presumably to shave bytes, since only this site's
    own JS ever needs to decode it back. Order matters: decode
    &q;/&l;/&g; before &a;, so that the literal '&' produced by
    decoding &a; doesn't accidentally get re-matched on the same pass.
    """
    return (text.replace("&q;", '"')
                .replace("&l;", "<")
                .replace("&g;", ">")
                .replace("&a;", "&"))
