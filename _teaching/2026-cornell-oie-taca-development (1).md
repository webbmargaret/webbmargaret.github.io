Role in the Cornell Engineering Office of Inclusive Excellence (OIE) TA/CA Development Program, co-developing and co-facilitating evidence-based, interactive training for engineering graduate Teaching Assistants (TAs) and undergraduate Course Assistants (CAs) under Dr. Celia Evans and Dr. Wenjing Luo. This program is a central mechanism for building inclusive instructional capacity across Cornell Engineering, and I also conduct research on the program's design and outcomes.

## Program Structure

The OIE TA/CA Development Program operates across three tracks with distinct formats and audiences:

**Track 1: OIE Leads — Weekly Workshop Series (May–August)**
PhD and MS students who are experienced TAs becoming consultant-trainers. The program runs ~9 weekly workshops from May through August (with July as an independent implementation month), structured around a repeating cycle of train → implement → return → debrief. This structure creates natural, repeated opportunities for OIE Leads to surface and reflect on teaching tensions they encounter in practice.

**Track 2: Graduate TAs — 2-Day Intensive + Microteaching (August)**
All new MS/PhD TAs are required to attend a two-day intensive training (Aug 20–21) covering inclusive teaching practices, grading, Universal Design for Learning (UDL), and active learning. I facilitate workshops during this event and then conduct **microteaching sessions** with small groups of TAs during the first two weeks of the semester — providing structured, one-on-one reflective feedback on early teaching practice at the most critical point in a new TA's semester.

**Track 3: Masters/Undergraduate CAs — Half-Day Training (September)**
Masters and undergraduate CAs attend a half-day training event (Sept 8, week 3 of classes) covering inclusive practices, active learning, professionalism, grading, and group dynamics.

## Pedagogical Approach: Critical Tensions Framework

A distinctive feature of my contribution to this program is the application of a **critical tensions framework** developed from my own postdoctoral research on DBER professional development (see [*Building Capacity for Instructional Change*](https://webbmargaret.github.io/publication/2026-01-01-building-capacity-for-instructional-change-dber-postdoctoral-professional-development)). 

"Critical tensions" are uncomfortable but necessary learning experiences — moments of mismatch between what an emerging educator expects and what actually happens — that can lead to growth when navigated with relational support, or to disengagement when left unaddressed. TAs and CAs are structurally analogous to the postdocs in my research: they occupy dual roles as both learners and contributors, hold limited positional authority, and depend on relational scaffolding from supervisors and peers.

Rather than designing around these friction moments, my approach **builds them in on purpose**:

- Embedding **vignette-based scenario activities** in trainings that surface predictable tension types (e.g., group dynamics that don't match what was practiced in training, student pushback to active learning, navigating course faculty expectations) and model productive responses
- Designing **structured reflection prompts** for microteaching debriefs that ask TAs to name a friction moment, describe their response, and identify the support they sought
- Framing the program as preparation for navigating difficulty — not just skill acquisition — so that when tensions arise during the semester, TAs/CAs have language and strategies ready

## Evidence of Effectiveness

**Microteaching Sessions (Fall 2026, n = 9 graduate TAs in sessions I facilitated)**

| Post-session survey item | Strongly Agree / Agree |
|---|---|
| The session fulfilled its stated objectives | 9 of 9 (6 strongly agree) |
| The session provided valuable feedback on my teaching | 9 of 9 (8 strongly agree) |
| The session gave me more ideas I can use | 9 of 9 (7 strongly agree) |
| The session should be offered next year | 8 of 9 |

Asked what their facilitator did well, TAs most often named the climate and the quality of feedback — that I "broke the ice quickly," "created a warm environment," and "made sure everyone got feedback." The survey also included the critical-tensions reflection prompt described above, and TAs' answers show the framework doing its work: new TAs named friction moments such as running out of time, a polling tool failing mid-lesson, or worrying their topic wouldn't land — and described how they responded in the moment (troubleshooting, checking understanding with more questions, adding an analogy). The most common suggestion — more than seven minutes per teaching segment and smaller groups — is informing next year's session design.

**Group Dynamics and Processes Workshop (Graduate TA and Undergraduate CA Trainings)**

I co-designed and facilitated the case-based Group Dynamics and Processes session for both the graduate TA intensive and the CA training. Participants analyzed realistic group-work scenarios (e.g., a dominating team member, free-riding in a multi-week lab project, a neurodivergent student struggling with the pace of their group) and practiced strategies such as rotating roles, structured round-robins, and second-chance voting.

- **Knowledge check:** On an embedded concept question about why intentional team processes improve group performance, **88% of graduate TAs (65 of 74)** and **90% of undergraduate CAs (66 of 73)** selected the correct answer. Scenario-based items showed more varied responses, which we used to identify strategies needing more explicit modeling.
- **Participant feedback (57 open-ended responses):** TAs and CAs most valued the case studies, the concrete strategy handout, and the interactive role practice. One TA explained that the interactive component "made it easy for me to imagine how I could incorporate what I learned into my own teaching"; a CA preparing to run a multi-week lab project called it "very relevant to what decisions I will have to make."
- **Responding to critical feedback:** Some CAs found parts of the session repetitive or less relevant to office-hours roles, and a few found the slides too lecture-heavy. For the next cycle, we are shortening the lecture portion, differentiating scenarios by CA role (lab, recitation, office hours), and expanding time for worked solutions — applying the same continuous-improvement loop I ask TAs to use in their own teaching.

## Research on the Program

I am also conducting research on the TA/CA Development Program as a site for studying how emerging educators develop teaching agency through relational structures. Research questions guiding this work include:

1. What critical tensions do student-teachers in engineering higher education experience in their teaching development?
2. How do OIE Leads who recognize and name critical tensions show different agency trajectories over time?
3. Do TAs/CAs who receive tension-framing in training report more self-efficacy when friction hits, compared to previous cohorts?
4. Does the relational scaffolding built into the OIE Leads cohort structure buffer disengagement compared to a "workshop and drop" approach?

Data collection spans longitudinal reflection prompts with OIE Leads, pre/post assessments with TA and CA cohorts, microteaching debrief prompts, and optional mid- and end-of-semester semi-structured interviews.
