Doctoral education in engineering education research (EER) involves more than mastering content and methods — it requires navigating a set of unwritten expectations, informal norms, and tacit knowledge that are rarely made explicit in formal curricula. This chapter examines the "hidden curriculum" of the PhD: the unstated rules and practices that shape how students are socialized into the field, from early stages of program entry through dissertation completion. Drawing on cross-institutional perspectives from researchers trained in the US and Finland, we surface common patterns in how doctoral students learn to identify research problems, build scholarly identity, navigate advisor relationships, manage the emotional labor of research, and position themselves for careers in EER. We argue that making the hidden curriculum visible is not only an equity issue — it disproportionately disadvantages students without prior exposure to academic culture — but also a field-building imperative, as EER's interdisciplinary and methodologically diverse nature makes tacit knowledge especially hard to transfer. The chapter offers practical guidance for doctoral students, advisors, and program designers seeking to make PhD training in EER more transparent, equitable, and effective.
