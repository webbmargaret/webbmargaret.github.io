As the world becomes more digitized, information and communication technologies (ICTs) are more embedded in daily life in the United States– not just at an individual level but also within government institutions and practices. The incorporation of ICTs into the citizenship application and preparation process was originally conceived with the idea that ICTs promise to help facilitate democratic participation for vulnerable groups. However, our work suggests that the proliferation of these technologies might create more barriers than supports to the meaningful integration for people seeking refuge (PSR). Current approaches to incorporating ICTs into U.S. citizenship processes only amplifiy existing knowledge-based inequities, and designs based on dominant narratives of integration do not respond to PSR’s lived experiences, fostering issues of digital injustice for newcomers in this country. 
The structural nature of these problems, combined with ongoing trends toward ICT proliferation in government processes highlight the importance of taking a critical look at how resettled communities experience naturalization online, as well as the extent to which PSRs experiences, values, and expertise inform technological development in these processes. There is a lack of research on the limitations of existing ICT practices for PSRs applying for citizenship, and there is even less research driven by refugee and immigrant communities towards actually addressing ICTs incorporations’ systemic effects on democratic participation.
Research Questions and Study Purpose
We took a qualitative, community-engaged and participatory action research approach aligned with critical displacement methodology and grounded in theories of knowledge justice, digital justice, and epistemic integration for this work. Our efforts were aimed at reimagining inequitable citizenship structures and extending interdisciplinary conversations that enchance technologies to support human thriving. These perspectives guided our development of ICTs with digital justice at the core, all informed by community-stated needs and definitions of meaningful integration. We sought to answer these research questions:
1.	What knowledge, experiences, and/or counter-expertise of PSRs is not represented in the design of current digitized versions of the US naturalization process and related preparation materials?
2.	What normative assumptions about migrants and integration are embedded in these US naturalization processes and associated resources online, and how do these structural assumptions impact PSRs’ digital justice and epistemic integration?
