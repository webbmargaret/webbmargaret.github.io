We have been developing a framework for understanding graduate students' and postdocs' experiences of "critical tensions"— challenging learning situations that are necessary for professional development. These emerging scholars can hold dual roles as both learners and expert contributors, working alongside faculty supervisors. We argue that navigating critical tensions as grad students and postdocs therefore happens in relationships, as scholars-in-the-making seek recognition and guidance from established scholars.

Understanding these critical tensions requires examining perspectives of both the emerging scholars and faculty supervisors. However, presenting these stories raises serious de-identification challenges, especially when describing each stakeholders' assessments of one another's contributions. Research fiction methods offer a solution: fictionalized case studies are well-established in higher education for protecting identities in research, training practitioners, and fostering debate about complex and difficult-to-discuss issues. As such, we are also developing evidence-based methods for fictionalizing institutional identifiers, structures, and situations (including critical tensions and interactions in graduate student/postdoc–faculty relationships) in research data while maintaining transparent quality and using Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI).

In this work, we present one fictionalized story derived from our larger study of nine postdoc-faculty interviews as well as four GenAI-enabled synthetic vignettes. We present the composite narrative authored by human researchers as a research-fiction artifact meant to be read and discussed, outline our human and GenAI-enabled fictionalization approaches, and then apply our theoretical framework to surface the critical tensions embedded in the stories. We end the human-authored fictionalized narrative with discussion questions that invite the audience to sit with ambiguity, compare interpretations, and use the narrative to catalyze thoughtful conversation about how developing scholars experience—and are supported through—these tensions. We also outline our process for generating synthetic vignettes based on fictionalized composites using GenAI, providing a prompt useful in generic GenAI chat environments and a rubric for human evaluation of the quality of synthetic vignettes. We present these four synthetic vignettes and a GenAI research vignette fictionalization rubric as a work-in-progress meant to be discussed and used in further refinement.
