Background
Interdisciplinary graduate education (IGE) operates through a complex ecosystem of departments, disciplines, research groups, and cultures. Within this ecosystem, despite decades of policy initiatives and hundreds of millions of dollars invested to cultivate interdisciplinary scholars, engineering graduate students continue to struggle with developing and maintaining interdisciplinary identities and motivation.
These struggles suggest the need to move beyond isolated programmatic interventions and employ systems-level approaches to understand the IGE ecosystem.
Purpose/Hypothesis(es)
This study employs Ecological Systems Theory to examine how salient microsystems interact to shape engineering graduate students’ experiences of IGE. Specifically, we ask how the IGE microsystems shape students’ identity-based motivation.
Design/Method
This study is the third phase of a longitudinal qualitative case study of 8 engineering graduate students in an NSF-funded interdisciplinary program. We analyzed interviews with graduate students and program faculty to identify microsystem interactions and characterize their impacts on students’ identity-based motivation. Using deductive coding, pattern coding, and visualization techniques, we identified salient patterns and themes.
Results
We identified four primary microsystem interaction patterns: Supportive Reinforcements, Compounded Barriers, Negotiation-Intensive Contradictions, and Transformative Tensions. Notably, we found that interactions occurred across time, with both past and future microsystems impacting present experiences. Similarly, interactions often had cascading impacts across multiple microsystems.
Conclusions
Because of the interactions across microsystems, sustainable change in IGE requires coordinated action across multiple stakeholders to strategically align system functions, identify leverage points at generative system interactions, and develop networked resilience that spans institutional and external partnerships.
